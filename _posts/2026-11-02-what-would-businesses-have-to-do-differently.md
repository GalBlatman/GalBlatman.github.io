I'm on the academic job market right now, hoping to become a business school professor. When I tell people outside academia that I study sustainability, I sometimes get a puzzled look. How does that fit into a business school?

I've asked myself that too. And I keep coming back to the same answer: sustainability is a business question. Companies need to figure out how to stay competitive over time. Using fewer resources, preparing for changing markets, and making investments that will pay off years from now are all part of that. Not every sustainable investment makes financial sense, of course. But figuring out which ones do, and why, is exactly the kind of thing business schools should study.

Christopher Marquis recently argued in the *New York Times* that business schools should be shut down. If anything, questions like these are why I think we need them.

I recently read two articles that got me thinking about this from a different angle.

Eoin McLaughlin, writing in *Nature*, asks whether economic growth actually leaves future generations better off. A country can keep growing its GDP while using up the natural resources that helped it grow in the first place. So maybe we should pay more attention to what we're leaving behind, not just what we're producing today.

Giorgos Kallis and colleagues ask something a little different in *The Lancet Planetary Health*. Do we really need economies to keep growing for people's lives to keep improving?

I expected to disagree with them more than I did. Growth has helped lift billions of people out of poverty, and I don't think anyone should dismiss that. The authors have some pretty different ideas about how economies should work, and I don't agree with all of them. But their basic question seems fair. Once a country is wealthy enough that producing more doesn't do much to improve people's lives, should growing the economy still be its main goal?

One of the proposals in the Kallis review is a shorter working week. I'm not exactly the best spokesperson for that idea. I probably wouldn't have finished my PhD without working 80-hour weeks.

But say a company is asked to reduce working hours without cutting salaries. Employees get more free time, which sounds great. Except the company is now paying more for every hour worked. It might respond by automating some of those jobs instead. Think about the current conversation around AI. Suddenly, a policy meant to protect employment could end up costing people their jobs.

That's what I find interesting. You can have a good idea about what society should do, but companies might respond in ways you didn't expect.

This is close to what I study. Companies face demands from investors, regulators, customers, and other groups that don't always agree. I'm interested in what companies actually change in response, and why those changes aren't always what the people making the demands had in mind.

Think about a company considering an investment that would substantially reduce its emissions. Maybe it would also save money, and the decision would be pretty easy. But what if it doesn't? What if the equipment is expensive and the savings won't cover the cost? Or maybe it pays off eventually, just not on the timeline the company's investors expect.

From the company's perspective, turning it down might be perfectly reasonable. The environmental benefit could be real, but someone has to pay for it.

Now suppose the rules change. Maybe customers are willing to pay more for the cleaner product. Maybe lenders offer better financing, or regulations make emissions more expensive. The same investment could suddenly make financial sense.

That's where the institutional side of sustainability comes in. Companies don't decide entirely on their own what counts as good performance. Investors, lenders, regulators, customers, and rating agencies all have a say. Their expectations influence which investments get funded, what managers are rewarded for, and what companies can afford to do.

I guess that's what I took from these articles. We can change what we want from the economy, but if we keep rewarding companies for the same things, why would we expect them to behave differently?
